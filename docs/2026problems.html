Intersecting intervals | 13/04/2026
An interval on $[0, 1]$ is generated by sampling two points uniformly at random and taking the interval between them. If $5$ such intervals are generated, what is the probability that they have a common intersection?

Solution

Wrong solution
Imagine that such an interval exists, and you are standing on it. Then all the left endpoints must lie to your left and all the right endpoints must lie to your right. Let us denote the left endpoints as points $1$ to $5$ and the right endpoints as points $6$ to $10$. In addition, let points $1$ and $6$ correspond to the first interval, points $2$ and $7$ to the second interval, and so on. Then for any $10$ positions, the number of ways that $1$ to $5$ can lie to your left and $6$ to $10$ to your right is $5!5!$. Since there are $10!$ ways to arrange the $10$ points, the probability is $\frac{5!5!}{10!} = \frac{1}{252}$.

Unfortunately, this is wrong. Do you see why? The reason is that the $10!$ arrangements contain some arrangements that are illegal! We have designated points $1$ to $5$ to be left endpoints and points $6$ to $10$ to be right endpoints, but in some arrangements we might have say point $1$ on the right of point $6$.

Resolution
To point ourselves in the right direction, let's consider the following problem. Given $N$ pairs of objects $(A_1, B_1), \dots, (A_N, B_N)$, how many ways are there to arrange the $2N$ objects such that for each $i$, $A_i$ is to the left of $B_i$? We can approach this problem by recursion. Let $f(N)$ denote the number of valid arrangements for $N$ pairs. Take any of $A_1, \dots, A_N$ and place it on the leftmost spot. Call this choice $A_k$. Then $B_k$ can go into any of the remaining $2N-1$ spots. Having placed $A_k$ and $B_k$, we are left with $N-1$ pairs of objects to arrange, which can be done in $f(N-1)$ ways. Therefore, we have the recursion $f(N) = N \cdot (2N-1) \cdot f(N-1)$, and since $f(1) = 1$, we can solve this to get $f(N) = N! \cdot \prod_{k=1}^N (2k-1)$. This gives $f(5) = 113400$. So the total number of valid arrangements is $113400$, not $10! = 3628800$. Therefore, the probability is $\frac{5!5!}{113400} = \boxed{\frac{8}{63}}$.
